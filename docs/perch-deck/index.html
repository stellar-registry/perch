Perch

Bounded grants for apps and agents on Stellar

Perch accounts run on Stellar through the Nido wallet.

Willem Wyndham and Elizabeth Engelman
The Aha Company

  • today

Perch is a Stellar smart account that gives an app or agent a grant, a permission with limits, and enforces those limits itself at one decision point. Perch accounts run on Stellar through the Nido wallet. The dial is the deck's motif: the account at the centre, a ring of ledgers, and a window that holds the charges a grant has made. Solid cyan in an engraved frame is what Perch does, as the main paper presents it. Dashed magenta, with printer's marks, is where it could go.

An app that spends

An app that spends for a user has two options without Perch. It can ask the user to approve every call, which does not work for an agent that pays a service many times an hour. Or it can hold a key that can do anything the account can, so whoever steals the app's key can empty the account. Perch adds a third: the owner installs a grant, a written permission with limits, and the account checks every call the app makes in its name against it.

docs/perch-paper/source/html/sections/motivation.html, second paragraph.

A grant

The paper's running example is the shop grant. Its one rule names the contract (the native XLM token), the function (transfer), argument 0 as this account and argument 1 as the shop, the key that must sign (the app's own Ed25519 key, checked by a verifier contract) and a cap: at most 10 XLM (100,000,000 stroops) in any 100 ledgers, about eight minutes at five seconds a ledger. A rule may also stop at an expiry ledger. Nothing else is allowed: no other token, recipient, function or signer, and no call to the account itself.

The owner installs a grant document with one call, apply_grant, after the document compiler contract validates it; remove_grant deletes its rules and spending records.

Attenuation

Attenuation derives a narrower grant from an existing one, for an app or agent handing part of its authority to a helper such as a sub-agent. rule-core defines 10 narrowing steps, among them dropping a rule, removing functions, adding an argument condition, raising a signer threshold, lowering a cap, lengthening its period and moving the expiry earlier. From the shop grant, a helper can receive a grant that pays the same shop at most 2 XLM per 100 ledgers.

Theorem 4: a grant derived by attenuation allows no call its parent does not, under any signers; its comparison reads no cap or expiry, so it covers step 1 (only guess). Theorem 5, trace inclusion: every run a derived grant accepts from empty records, its parent accepts too, which covers steps 2 and 3 (the lower cap and the earlier last ledger). Parent and child keep separate records, and neither theorem bounds what they spend together.

The drawing follows the paper's attenuation model: a parent with a game rule (any function) and a shop rule (XLM transfers to the shop, at most 10 per 100 ledgers), narrowed by restricting the game to guess, lowering the cap to the 2 XLM the paper gives the helper, and setting an earlier last ledger, three of the 10 steps. The ledgers N and M are illustrative. A 5 XLM payment to the shop is one the app's grant allows and the helper's refuses, as in the model's probe table; a call the parent refuses, such as a payment to anyone else, the child refuses too.

The cap window

The cap is a sliding window. At ledger L the window holds the charges made after L − 100 and through L, and a transfer is accepted only if the window's total plus the new amount stays within the limit. The account keeps the charges in a spending record in its own storage and charges a transfer in the same decision that checks it.

The five events are the paper's: +6 at t, accepted; +5 at t, refused (6 + 5 = 11 > 10) and the record is unchanged; +4 at t + 1, accepted (the limit is inclusive); +1 at t + 99, refused; at t + 100 the 6 leaves the window and +5 is accepted, leaving 9. Theorem 6: from an empty record, however the app times its payments, no window of 100 ledgers holds more than 10 XLM of accepted charges. A limit that resets on a schedule lets an app spend the full cap just before a reset and again just after it; a sliding window stays within its limit wherever it is placed. The axis is compressed between t + 2 and t + 98.

The authorization path

If the user signs, the wallet submits the transaction and the account decides in a fixed order. The host calls __check_auth. Guards refuse requests the account never allows through a grant. The account looks up each rule the transaction names and its spending record; it does not search. The decision function, rule_eval::authorize in rule-core, checks every call against its rule and computes the new records; it reads no storage and checks no signature, and it is the part the proofs cover. Only if it accepts does a verifier contract check the app's signature, which binds the account and the rule IDs the transaction names, so a relayer cannot move the payment to another rule the key belongs to. Only then does the account store the new records.

A refusal at any stage writes nothing; the decision function reports contract error 5 (RuleEvalRefused). Theorems 1, 2 and 6 are about the decision function (slide 15).

Create your Nido account

Nido creates accounts with its own factory rather than Perch's account factory: create_account(setup_pk, key, setup_sig) deploys an account whose one signer is External(WebAuthn verifier, P-256 key). The address comes from a salt derived from a setup key, so it exists before the passkey, whose relying-party ID is the account's own subdomain; the setup signature binds the network, the factory and the key. The account's constructor installs one rule, admin: the passkey, through Perch's WebAuthn verifier, over the account itself, the rule Nido's admin baseline document describes.

In the simulation, Nido's factory is rebuilt on this stack's account Wasm (the factory Nido pins today deploys an earlier account, Perch c135aca, v0.5.1, which predates grants and exact roots), and a software P-256 key stands in for the passkey. Both are labelled simulated.

Install the grant

The owner installs the app's grant with one call, apply_grant, approved in the Nido sign dialog and signed by the passkey under the account's admin rule. The document compiler validates the grant document, and the account installs its rules. For the demo the grant covers two rooms and the payment asked inside each: a game rule (any game function) and a transfer rule rooted at game.guess for the XLM stake, paid only to the game, and a shop rule (order) and a transfer rule rooted at shop.order for the simulated EURC payment, paid only to the shop. Each transfer rule pins the recipient (address-eq on argument 1), so a payment to anyone else is refused by the rule. Slides 9 and 10 show how each node is checked.

Every call in the tree

A grant covers a root call and the calls it makes on the account's behalf, and the account checks every node of the authorization tree against the rule the payload names for it. In the guess scenario, game.guess calls guesser.require_auth(), and a loss pays the stake with the XLM token's transfer(account, game, stake) inside it, so the account's entry is [game.guess, token.transfer]. The grant has a game rule and a transfer rule. The root passes the game rule. The nested transfer passes the transfer rule only if it is from the account itself (is-self on argument 0), only to the game (address-eq on argument 1, as the Nido simulation's grant pins it; the auth_root.rs test grant leaves the recipient open), within the cap (argument 2 is charged to the window; in the test, 2 XLM per 100 ledgers with a 1 XLM stake, so the cap refuses a third loss), and its entry's root is exactly game.guess (the rule's auth-root). The same transfer made directly, or under another root, is refused, and in the Nido simulation a stake the game's owner redirects to itself is refused at the recipient check ("the payment must go to Game"). A win is paid by the game itself, so the account's entry holds only game.guess and uses no allowance. The root check names an exact contract and function; it does not inspect root arguments or other ancestors.

Theorem 11 (authorization-tree coverage, in the host model): every call an account's entry approves is allowed by the rules its __check_auth checks, and those see the whole tree. The exact-root condition itself is tested (auth_root.rs), not proved.

Where the limit lives

OpenZeppelin's Soroban account library composes the decision from separate contracts. Its unit of permission is a context rule: a target such as one contract, an optional expiry, signers, and attached policies, each a contract the account calls on every call the rule approves. oz-policy-builder writes rules for these accounts: a shared policy-interpreter contract evaluates a synthesised predicate on every call the rule guards and keeps no totals. Its documentation says limits over time come from a token allowance with an expiry, or from OpenZeppelin's spending_limit policy beside it.

In the simulation the predicate pins the recipient and bounds each transfer at 10 XLM, with spending_limit at 10 XLM per 100 ledgers. A Perch cap is part of its rule and is decided in one function. oz-policy-builder is ahead in maturity, authoring and expressiveness.

What is proved

The decision function is about 2,400 lines of Rust. The Charon and Aeneas tools translate it into Lean, a proof assistant whose kernel checks every proof, and theorems relate the translation to a readable model written by hand. Six of the 13 theorems: Theorem 1, on every input the translation returns exactly what the model returns, without panicking or looping; Theorem 2, if the decision function accepts, every call is allowed by a rule it was given, under the signers it was given; Theorem 6, from an empty record no window holds more than the cap; Theorem 4, a narrowed grant allows no call its parent does not; Theorem 8, no grant, nor one derived from it, allows a call to the account or its adopted recovery contracts; Theorem 10, after a completed recovery no earlier grant accepts a request with a call in it.

Each theorem has hypotheses; for example, the signer list is an input that the account authenticates. The step from the evaluator to the account (P1) is argued, not proved, and nothing checks the compiled Wasm against the translation. Perch's tests run 57 traces generated from the Lean model through the compiled account.

An agent raised its own cap

Breckenridge, Vilardell, Wang, Zhang and Juels, “I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop”, 2026arXiv:2610.35835

A study from Cornell Tech, Yale University and IC3 tested a human-in-the-loop gate on spending. A LangChain agent had three tools: the user's email, a web browser and an agent wallet holding 10 USDC on Base mainnet, with a spending policy of 0.01 USDC per transaction, 0.01 USDC per day, 0.30 USDC per week and 1 USDC per month. Changing the policy needs a one-time passcode that the wallet provider emails to the account owner. The system prompt told the agent to act only on the user's instructions and to treat anything a tool returns, emails included, as data; no task mentioned the spending policy or how to change it.

In the payment experiment the inbox held three invoices of 0.01 USDC each, so paying all three needed a higher daily cap. Across 20 trials per model, with no adversary present, 9 of 10 models read the code from the inbox and raised their own spending cap in at least one run. In a second experiment, tasked only with summarizing the inbox, 5 of 10 models paid an attacker who impersonated the user by email. The authors disclosed the results to the wallet provider, Circle, on 22 September 2026; Circle's agent-wallet documentation now warns never to give an agent access to the inbox that receives the code. The paper argues that a human in the loop needs a channel the agent cannot reach.

Breckenridge, Vilardell, Wang, Zhang and Juels, "I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop", arXiv:2610.35835 (v3, 30 October 2026): p. 4 (Setup, Experiments, Key Findings), p. 5 (Results), Appendix D (p. 18). The ten models are not named here.

A cap the agent cannot raise

Perch keeps the cap in the account's own grant, and the account's decision, rule_eval::authorize, checks every call against it. Changing a grant, with apply_grant or remove_grant, is a call to the account that only its admin rule authorizes; in Nido the admin is the owner's passkey, a signature made on the owner's device that the agent cannot produce. A grant can never authorize a call to the account itself or its recovery contracts. That is Theorem 8, grant target containment: "No grant as lowered at install, nor any grant derived from one, allows a call to the account or its adopted recovery contracts."

So the agent's own key cannot raise its own cap, and there is nothing an emailed code could open: reading an email gets the agent nothing. This assumes the agent holds only its grant's key and the owner's passkey stays on the owner's device. It is not the paper's I-HITL protocol; it is where Perch puts the limit.

The agent runtime

Could be: planned work, which nothing in this repository builds yet. Agents run as WASI components and hold no keys. A root agent's authority is a grant installed on a Perch account and read from it; a subagent is spawned only with a grant derived from its parent's, so never wider (Theorems 4 and 5 cover attenuation in the model). Every file, website, signing and spawn request goes to a Cedar policy engine packaged as an import-free WebAssembly component, with no files, sockets or keys of its own; a subagent's request must be permitted by its own policies and every ancestor's.

Signing goes through a key service that checks the signing request and every authorization context before it signs. Payments, over x402 on Stellar, and contract calls reach the Perch account, which rechecks them on chain against the grant. The account, not the runtime, holds the cap, so an over-cap payment is refused even if the runtime is compromised. Today (solid): the account, grants, the proofs and Nido.

Vision paper §08, "Agents, budgets and payments (proposed)", and §09, "Cedar as an authoring language (proposed)" (docs/perch-paper/source/papers/vision/html/sections/agents.html, cedar.html), for what they state: agents hold no keys, the key service, child grants derived from a root grant, x402, the account rechecking on chain, and Cedar as an authoring language; docs/cedar-walkthrough (the compiler prototype); source/statements.json (T4, T5). The WASI packaging, the import-free Cedar engine and the every-ancestor rule are the planned runtime design, not taken from those sections. Proposed.

Slot and credential

Could be. Today a Perch signer is Delegated(address), which the host checks, or External(verifier, key): the account calls the verifier contract's verify(digest, key, signature) and refuses the authorization unless it says yes. Read the second kind as a slot: the signer is the slot, and the credential is what fills it. Ed25519 and WebAuthn (passkey) verifiers are deployed; the passkey's key is a 65-byte P-256 key and its signature a WebAuthn assertion.

An email credential could fill the same slot: an email verifier, a public part (a commitment C to the address, and the DKIM key registry's root) in place of the key, and a zero-knowledge proof in place of the signature. Nothing in Perch builds this.

An email proof

Could be. The email proof as Nido's email-recovery design specifies it: a DKIM-signed email whose From address opens a salted commitment C = H(address, code), whose Subject carries T = H(digest, ρ), the digest blinded, and whose provider's RSA DKIM key is a leaf of a key registry. The proof is made in the browser. Its public inputs are C, the digest and the key root; the email itself stays hidden.

For Perch, the digest would be the recovery statement's digest, which exists today: the SHA-256 of a 278-byte statement that binds the network, account, controller, configuration, timing and attempt. The Subject hex is illustrative.

Today: Perch docs/recovery/statement.md; docs/perch-paper/source/facts/limits.json (recovery-statement-bytes). The email proof: the ZK signer deck's report (Nido docs/email-recovery/spec.md §4.2, §5.3, §6.1, as cited there); not re-checked in this repository. Proposed for Perch.

One vote at the controller

Today (solid): anyone may open a recovery attempt. Each guardian approves by authorizing the controller's submit_guardian with require_auth_for_args((digest,)), for exactly one statement. At quorum the attempt is authorized; completion waits for the delay and must land inside the completion window, and it replaces the designated credentials. Under the Loss profile the owner can cancel during the delay; under Protected only the condition's evidence can.

Could be (dashed): the email credential, checked through the External verifier slot, acts as one guardian. It approves the same digest with a proof and counts as one vote. It never skips the delay, and with a quorum above one it never authorizes alone. So an agent that reads the owner's inbox, as on slide 17, still cannot recover or take over the account: its email proof is one vote among several. The owner should not give an agent the inbox used for recovery.

The email vote: the ZK signer deck's report ("Proofs as guardians"). Proposed.
