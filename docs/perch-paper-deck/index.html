The academic paper, in its own order, told through one picture. The account is a building with a master key, the owner's. A grant is a keycard with its limits printed on it: which doors (contracts and functions), how much it may spend in any window of ledgers, and when it expires. Cutting a narrower copy of a card is attenuation. The front desk checks every card before any lock turns. The proofs are an inspector certifying the lock design. Each sheet carries the paper section it follows.

Brass is the card and what it allows; blue is what a theorem proves; red is a refusal; dashed pencil is proposed.

docs/perch-paper/latex/perch-paper.tex; sections/abstract.tex; source/facts/paper.json (title, authors, affiliation, wallet-name).

Three ways to let an app spend

An app that spends for a user today either asks the user to approve every call or holds a key that can do anything. A Soroban account can be a contract that decides each authorization itself, so a middle ground exists: a grant lets an app or agent act for the account within limits the account itself enforces. The bound has to hold across the nested calls of one transaction, across repeated calls over time, and whichever installed rule the transaction's submitter selects.

Paper §1 (sections/motivation.tex), first paragraph.

The shop card

The running example, the shop grant (Fig. 1), lets an app or agent send XLM from the account to the shop and to no one else, at most 10 XLM in any 100 ledgers, about eight minutes. Its one rule names the native XLM token, the function transfer, argument 0 as this account and argument 1 as the shop, the app's Ed25519 key, and the cap. A grant cannot say everything: it has no bound on one call's amount (the cap bounds the window), no or and no not, no reads of other contracts, and it checks each call alone.

Paper §1 and Fig. 1 (sections/motivation.tex; source/figures/fig-shop-grant.a.txt, .b.txt); source/facts/example.json (shop-cap-xlm, shop-period-ledgers, shop-period-minutes).

Three bounds

"Safe and bounded" means three properties, not an audit: each call of an accepted request is allowed by an installed rule (Theorem 2), no window of a capped rule holds more than its limit (Theorem 6), and no grant allows a call to the account or its adopted recovery contracts (Theorem 8). "Proved" applies to the Lean translation of the Rust decision function, not the deployed Wasm or the tested account code around it, the shell. A completed recovery moves one counter, and every earlier grant stops authorizing.

Status: the design is an unmerged pull-request stack ending at #68, previewed on testnet through Nido, and not audited; the paper does not recommend it for real funds before an audit. An earlier design is on mainnet. soroban-sim is published by The Aha Company, where the authors work.

Paper §1 (sections/motivation.tex), "Safe and bounded" and "Status".

How the building is asked

A contract that needs an address's approval calls require_auth; for a contract account, the host then calls the account's __check_auth with the authorization entry's signed payload, its signatures and its contexts: every call and contract creation in the invocation tree, in preorder, each of which the account must decide. A lost guess in a game gives two, the game's guess and the transfer it makes from the account. The nonce is consumed only on success, so a refused entry can be resubmitted until it expires. Recording-mode simulation, which builds entries, does not call __check_auth, so only enforcing simulation predicts a refusal.

OpenZeppelin's Soroban library stores context rules with external signers, delegated signers and policies; in v0.8.0, __check_auth validates the selected rules, authenticates the signers, then calls each policy's enforce. oz-policy-builder's shared policy-interpreter evaluates a predicate on each guarded call alone and keeps no totals, which OpenZeppelin's spending_limit policy keeps. Perch reuses OpenZeppelin's types and payload field; it differs in what decides.

Paper §2 (sections/background.tex).

Issuing a card

The owner calls apply_grant with a grant document. A stateless document compiler validates the JSON; the account lowers it with rule-core's lower_scoped under its own address, its excluded set (its adopted recovery controller, and the zero-knowledge adapter and pool of its recovery configuration) and its grant generation, refusing any rule that targets the account or that set (Theorem 8).

A rule checks one target contract and a function list; five kinds of argument condition (is-self, address-eq, string-in, string-prefix, u32-eq), all of which must hold; a threshold over distinct listed signers; optionally an expiry (not-after-ledger N authorizes through ledger N − 1); and optionally a cap, when the function list is exactly ["transfer"]. The payload names one rule per context, the account does not search, and a signer that no chosen rule names is refused.

Paper §3 (sections/grants.tex), "Installing a grant" and "What a rule checks".

The allowance meter

For a cap of λ per P ledgers, the window at ledger L holds the charges made after L − P and through L. The decision that checks a call also charges it, contexts of one authorization that select the same rule see each other's charges, and a refusal changes nothing. The record lives in the account's storage and holds up to 907 spends; replacing a capped rule starts an empty window. Theorem 6 bounds every window from empty records.

The five events are Fig. 2's: +6 at t accepted; +5 at t refused (6 + 5 = 11 > 10, record unchanged); +4 at t + 1 accepted (the limit is inclusive); +1 at t + 99 refused; at t + 100 the 6 leaves and +5 is accepted, leaving 9. The axis is compressed between t + 2 and t + 98.

Paper §3 and Fig. 2 (sections/grants.tex; source/figures/fig-cap-window.steps.json); source/facts/example.json (cap-event-1 to cap-event-5); source/facts/limits.json (spends-per-window).

Cutting a copy

Attenuation, modelled and not installable: a grant derived for a helper by rule-core's 10 attenuation steps allows no call its parent does not (Theorem 4). Caps and expiry narrow by trace inclusion (Theorem 5 of the verification companion), and neither bounds what parent and child spend together. The steps: drop a rule; remove or restrict functions; add an argument condition; remove signers or raise a threshold; add a cap, lower its limit or lengthen its period; set or lower the last ledger. No step changes a rule's target or generation. Installing an attenuated grant is proposed (§12).

Paper §3 (sections/grants.tex, "Attenuation (modelled; not installable)"); Appendix B, Definition 5 and Theorem 4 (sections/repro.tex; source/statements.json T4).

When a card stops

A grant stops at its expiry, by one owner call (remove_grant, which deletes its storage), or, with every earlier grant, when a recovery completes: a completed recovery moves one counter, and every earlier grant stops authorizing. Anyone may then remove a stale grant's storage with gc_grant, which refuses a current-generation grant. Recovery invalidation (G4) is proved in an account model (Theorem 10 of the verification companion), tested and run on testnet.

Paper §3 (sections/grants.tex, "Ending a grant"); §1; §5 (sections/threat-model.tex, "Goals and trust").

One card, every node

Beyond the paper: exact entry roots arrived in the stack with commit c593bac ("constrain rules to exact entry roots"), after the paper's pin f0275f4; the paper lists "rules that require their entry's root" as proposed in §12.

Every node of an authorization tree is checked against the rule the payload names for it. In the guess scenario, game.guess calls guesser.require_auth(), and a loss pays the stake with the XLM token's transfer(account, game, stake) inside it, so the account's entry is [game.guess, token.transfer]. The grant has a game rule and a transfer rule. The root passes the game rule. The nested transfer passes the transfer rule only if it is from the account itself (is-self on argument 0), only to the game (address-eq on argument 1, as the Nido simulation's grant pins it; the auth_root.rs test grant leaves the recipient open), within the cap (argument 2 is charged to the window; in the test, 2 XLM per 100 ledgers with a 1 XLM stake, so the cap refuses a third loss), and its entry's root is exactly game.guess (the rule's auth-root). The same transfer made directly, or under another root, is refused, and in the Nido simulation a stake the game's owner redirects to itself is refused at the recipient check ("the payment must go to Game"). A win is paid by the game itself, so the account's entry holds only game.guess and uses no allowance. The root check names an exact contract and function; it does not inspect root arguments or other ancestors.

Creating it: a one-time setup key gives the address from Nido's factory before any passkey exists; a passkey stand-in, a software P-256 key, is created; the setup key signs a binding of the network, the factory and the passkey; create_account deploys the account, whose constructor installs one admin rule for the passkey through Perch's WebAuthn verifier. Installing the grant: the passkey signs apply_grant under the admin rule, for a game rule, an XLM transfer rule rooted at game.guess that pays only the game (2 XLM per 100 ledgers), a shop rule (order), and a test-EURC transfer rule rooted at shop.order that pays only the shop (10 EURC per 100 ledgers). Each transfer rule pins the recipient (address-eq on argument 1), so a payment to anyone else is refused by the rule. Using it: a losing guess is the tree game.guess → XLM.transfer (1 XLM); a win is game.guess alone; a coffee is shop.order → EURC.transfer (3.50 EURC). Each request opens the Nido sign dialog, whose preview runs the request in the page first; after approval the page shows each node's checks. Simulated, and labelled so: the passkey stand-in, Nido's factory rebuilt to deploy this stack's account, the EURC test token, and the game and shop written for the demo. The front desk

Figure 3 follows one authorization through six stages: the account decides at one point, writes nothing on a refusal, and calls the verifier only after the decision accepts. Stage 4 is rule-core's rule_eval::authorize: it returns the next spending records or a refusal, reads no storage and checks no signature (Definition 3 is its model). The account reports refusals as auth error 3 (a stale grant) or 5 (any other). Stage 4 is the function the theorems cover; the other stages are tested account code, and the host is trusted.

What a signature binds: an external signer signs a digest of the host's payload, this account and the selected rule IDs. A delegated signer approves the host's payload, which omits the rule IDs, so a submitter may select any rule that lists the delegate; OpenZeppelin v0.8.0 binds them for both kinds.

Paper §4 and Fig. 3 (sections/authorization.tex; source/figures/fig-auth-path.steps.json).

Who holds the card

A grant has to hold against the app that holds it and against whoever submits its transactions: A1, the grantee's key, honest or stolen, and A2, a submitter or relayer. A1 can sign within rules naming its key, build any invocation tree, and choose among rules naming its key; what holds is Theorems 2, 6 and 8 (with signer authentication, selection and context conversion tested), and what remains is the union of authority across rules naming its key, caps per rule rather than per grant, each call checked alone, allowances and execute, approve through a token rule with no function list, and replacing a rule starting a new window. A2 can wrap signed entries, choose timing and fee, and resubmit a refused entry until its expiration ledger; an external signer's signature binds this account, the entry and the selected rule IDs (tested; a paper proof under signature and hash assumptions), while delegated signers do not bind rule IDs and a rule edited in place between signing and inclusion decides under its new contents.

Paper §5 and Table 1 (sections/threat-model.tex; source/tables/tab-grantee.json).

What the building trusts

Containment (G1), permission soundness (G2) and window safety (G3) are proved for lowering and the decision function, the code around them tested; fail-closed refusal (G6) is tested; recovery invalidation (G4) is proved in an account model (Theorem 10 of the verification companion), tested and run on testnet. The paper trusts the Soroban host and validators, the verifiers documents name, the adopted recovery controller, the document compiler, rustc and the wallet's display, and assumes unforgeable signature schemes (Ed25519; ECDSA P-256 for passkeys) and a collision-resistant SHA-256. Budgets across grants, effects outside authorization, confidentiality and verifier correctness are non-goals.

Paper §5 (sections/threat-model.tex, "Goals and trust").

Guards at the door, or one desk

Both designs build on OpenZeppelin's context rules, judge each call alone and deny by default. OpenZeppelin with oz-policy-builder composes a decision from contracts attached to a rule (interpreter, spending_limit, a threshold policy), each swappable and auditable on its own. Perch puts one document behind one decision point that holds every check and its state. Composition buys flexibility, such as a check that reads a live price, which a Perch rule cannot express; one decision point gives one place to check and to keep a limit's state. The interpreter "does not see what a transaction moved, keep rolling totals or count calls"; totals come from spending_limit, an opt-in policy that accepts transfer alone. A Perch window is part of the rule's decision (Theorem 6).

Paper §6 (sections/compare.tex), "Shared ground, two arrangements" and "A limit over time".

Where each is stronger

oz-policy-builder is ahead in maturity (its status, external review and tests on real transactions in Table 2), authoring (record or declare, synthesise a predicate, test that each conjunct is needed, and verify it) and expressiveness and ecosystem (reads of other contracts, quantifiers, clock windows, batches related through an execution adapter, threshold policies, a deny code per reason, and rules on existing OpenZeppelin accounts, where a grant needs a Perch account). Perch is ahead in limits over time, one decision point, which refuses before any verifier runs and installs one grant across contracts, and a proved core; no formal model was found in oz-policy-builder's tree, which is tested, partly on the shipped Wasm.

Five lessons for Perch: a front end that records a transaction and emits a grant document for review; simulating a grant against the account's own decision function before install; minimality checks, and overlap checks that refuse a cap another rule naming the same key bypasses; a corpus of real transactions; and an audit before any claim of maturity.

Paper §6 and Table 2 (sections/compare.tex; source/tables/tab-choose.json).

Open the simulation (4.9 MB, offline)

soroban-sim runs the contract code of both designs offline in one host. Each design gets a session on soroban-env-host 29.0.2 at protocol 29, applying with enforcing authorization in a recording-mode build: OpenZeppelin's v0.8.0 account with oz-policy-builder's policy-interpreter and spending_limit, or the Perch account with its document compiler and an Ed25519 verifier, each beside the native XLM contract, rebuilt from the pinned sources (only the OpenZeppelin side matches a deployment). The OpenZeppelin predicate pins the recipient and bounds each transfer at 10 XLM, with spending_limit adding 10 XLM per 100 ledgers; Perch runs the shop grant. The OpenZeppelin side signs as a delegated account, since oz-policy-builder refuses external signers on policed rules, hence 2 authorization entries to Perch's 1. Real contract bytes run offline and deterministically, every verdict carries a trace, and native, WASI and browser runs return byte-identical replies, timings removed. No instruction counts are reported.

The recorded run

A 6-XLM payment applies in both designs. The 5 XLM that follows is refused in both: Perch's trace shows __check_auth refusing with no verifier call, the OpenZeppelin account's shows spending_limit refusing (3221). With spending_limit removed, the OpenZeppelin account applies each of three 6-XLM sends under the per-call bound (18 XLM), as oz-policy-builder documents, while Perch refuses all three because its window holds 6. Re-adding spending_limit starts an empty history, as replacing a Perch rule starts an empty window. Another recipient, a forged signature and a key not on the rule are refused by both; 100 ledgers later, 5 XLM applies in both.

The inspector

The decision function is about 2,400 lines of no_std Rust in rule-core. Charon reads its MIR and Aeneas translates it into Lean; CI regenerates the translation, fails on any difference, and pins all 940 exported theorems to Lean's standard axioms. Proved means kernel-checked. The proofs trust the Lean kernel and its standard axioms, Charon, Aeneas and its Lean model of Rust primitives, rustc's MIR, and the specification, including the denotations.

Theorem 1 (Appendix B): on every input the translated evaluator succeeds, never panicking or diverging, and its value denotes the model's result on the denoted inputs; it says nothing about allocation failure or compiled Wasm.

Paper §8 (sections/proofs.tex), "The evaluator and its translation" and "What the proofs trust"; Appendix B, Theorem 1 (source/statements.json T1); source/facts/proofs.json (rule-eval-lines, exported-theorems).

Acceptance implies permission

Theorem 2: if evaluation accepts, the selected rule set allows every supplied context for the signer list it was given. The theorem takes that list as an input; the account authenticates it (obligation O1). Proved for the model and the translation.

Paper §8, Theorem 2 (sections/proofs.tex; source/statements.json T2).

Cap-window safety

Theorem 6: from empty records, in any run, counting its accepted authorizations, the charges made to a capped rule with a non-negative limit after L − P and through L sum to at most its limit, for every ledger L. The model also bounds every P consecutive ledgers that start at ledger 1 or later, and those from ledger 0 when no authorization is at ledger 0; without that hypothesis it fails from ledger 0. Proved for the model and the translation (the second part for the model only).

Paper §8, Theorem 6 (sections/proofs.tex; source/statements.json T6).

Grant target containment

Theorem 8: the rule set the account installs for a grant document, under its address, its excluded set and its generation, allows no call to the account or to an element of the excluded set, and attenuation preserves this. In the account, the excluded set is its adopted recovery controller and the zero-knowledge adapter and pool of its recovery configuration. Proved for the model and the translation, for rules whose argument conditions fit the evaluator's bounded vectors.

Paper §8, Theorem 8 (sections/proofs.tex; source/statements.json T8).

Evaluation

RQ1, enforcement: Perch's tests run 57 Lean-generated traces through the natively compiled account and check every verdict and spending record against the model. In the recorded run each Perch verdict is the one the shop grant specifies, and the designs agreed except on the three sends after the owner removed spending_limit.

Testnet preview: on 5 November 2026, a scripted run of Nido's preview with virtual passkeys made 11 testnet transactions, among them a game's two-rule grant, two lost guesses and a one-guardian recovery to completion; the wallet, not the chain, declined a third guess over the cap. Cost, Perch only: a passkey-signed transfer through a grant paid a testnet fee of 12,889 stroops, WebAuthn verification included; a fresh preview account holding the game's grant, after two capped transfers (3,308 bytes), pays 1.677 XLM a year in rent at the floor rate. Threats to validity: one task; one host version, recording-mode, without exact metering; signer kinds that differ by necessity; testnet resets, with envelopes not yet archived; and a game run from a branch with an uncommitted patch.

Paper §9 (sections/evaluation.tex); source/facts/testnet.json, proofs.json.

Limitations

The theorems concern the translation, not the Wasm rustc builds; the specification is trusted; the shell is tested; and no single theorem covers the whole authorization path. Table 1 lists what a grantee or a relayer can still do. Beyond it, a record holds at most 907 spends; two pre-signed installs for one grant can land in either order; and document caps (10 rules, 8 signers, 8,192 canonical bytes) bound what a grant can say. Nothing is audited; the owner key, recovery and the trusted components are in the vision companion.

Paper §10 (sections/limits.tex); source/facts/limits.json.

Related work

Hardy's confused deputy motivates designating authority explicitly, as capability systems do. SPKI/SDSI, macaroons, Biscuit and UCAN attenuate tokens a holder carries; a windowed cap needs past spending, which a token cannot carry without an external store, and in Schneider's terms makes the decision a stateful execution monitor. On EVM chains, ERC-4337 and ERC-7579 validators and hooks play the role of OpenZeppelin policies, ERC-7710 and ERC-7715 carry delegations and permission requests, and Zodiac Roles scopes a role to targets, functions and parameter conditions with refilling allowances. MetaMask's delegation framework caps transfers per fixed period, resetting at each period's start, so spends either side of a reset can reach twice the cap within one period's length; a Perch window (L − P, L] is bounded for every L (Theorem 6). In XACML's terms, Perch links its decision point into the account. Cedar links a Lean model to its Rust by differential testing; Perch proves a translation of its Rust equivalent to a model. oz-policy-builder's synthesis relates to policy mining. Sunbeam and Komet reason about compiled Wasm, which complements a proof about a translation.

Paper §11 (sections/related.tex).

Future work

Everything here is proposed: an agent runtime with delegation-tree budgets; an account entry point for attenuated grants; rules that require their entry's root (drafts Perch #69 and rule-core #17); and Cedar as an authoring language. The vision companion details each.

Paper §12 (sections/agents.tex).

Conclusion

Perch decides an app's grant at one point in the account and charges its spending window in that decision. The decision function's translation is proved to match a model and to keep the claim's three bounds; the shell is tested, and nothing is audited. In the recorded soroban-sim run, the two designs' verdicts differed only where the owner removed the limit over time. oz-policy-builder is ahead in maturity, authoring and expressiveness, and Perch should learn from it. What remains is to merge the stack, complete an audit, and measure both designs with exact metering.

Paper §13 (sections/conclusion.tex).
