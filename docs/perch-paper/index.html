Perch

Bounded grants for apps and agents on Stellar

A Stellar smart account that enforces an app's spending limits itself, at one decision point. Machine-checked proofs in the Lean proof assistant show that the decision accepts only what the grant allows and keeps every spending window within its cap.

For Soroban developers, wallet and app builders, and the people who decide how much an app or agent may do without asking.

Willem Wyndham and Elizabeth Engelman
The Aha Company
7 November 2026

ABSTRACT

Perch lets the owner of a Stellar smart account give an app or agent a grant: a permission naming the contracts it may call, whom it may pay, which keys must sign, and how much it may spend in any window of ledgers. The account checks every call against the grant at one decision point and keeps the spending record in its own storage, so the limit holds over time, not only per call. An app can hand a narrower grant to a helper, and recovering the account revokes every earlier grant at once. The decision function is translated into the Lean proof assistant, where machine-checked proofs show that it accepts only what the rules allow and keeps every window within its cap. In this page, soroban-sim runs Perch beside an OpenZeppelin account with oz-policy-builder, from their real contract code, offline.

Apps and agents need authority with limits

Perch lets an account give an app or agent authority whose limits the account itself enforces.

On Stellar, a smart account is a contract that holds a user's funds and approves the calls made in its name; Soroban is the platform that runs such contracts. An app that spends for a user today has two options. It can ask the user to approve every call, which does not work for an agent that pays a service many times an hour. Or it can hold a key that can do anything the account can, so that whoever steals the app's key can empty the account. Perch adds a third. The owner installs a grant, a written permission with limits, and the account checks every call the app makes in its name against it. Perch accounts run on Stellar through the Nido wallet. The overview diagram shows who does what.

This paper follows one request from start to finish:

  1. An app asks the user's Perch account to pay for an order.
  2. The sign dialog says in plain words what the request asks for, with the full tree of authority under Details.
  3. It previews the request's effects in the simulator.
  4. The user signs or refuses, and the account decides.
  5. The grant also covers the calls the request makes on the account's behalf, which a Nido account in the simulator shows.
  6. The same request runs against an OpenZeppelin account.

An app asks the account to pay for an order

Every request starts from a grant the owner installed. The paper's running example is the shop grant (the shop grant figure). It lets an app send XLM, Stellar's native currency, from the account to one shop and no one else, at most 10 XLM in any 100 ledgers, about eight minutes at about 5 seconds a ledger. The grant has one rule, which names the contract (the XLM token), the function (transfer), the recipient, the key that must sign (the app's) and a cap; a rule may also stop at an expiry ledger. The owner installs a grant document like this one with one call to the account, apply_grant, after a document compiler contract validates it. Wallets can build documents with the npm package @stellar-registry/perch.

The sign dialog in step 4 uses a similar grant from a toy café app: one rule that lets the café's key send XLM, capped at 10 XLM per 100 ledgers, with 3 XLM already spent. Unlike the shop grant, it does not pin the recipient. The café's checkout asks the account to pay for an order. The asker may also be a helper, such as a sub-agent, holding a narrower grant. Attenuation derives such a grant from the app's with rule-core's 10 narrowing steps, among them dropping a rule, removing functions, adding an argument condition, raising a signer threshold, lowering a cap, lengthening its period and moving the expiry earlier. From the shop grant, for example, a helper can receive a grant that pays the same shop at most 2 XLM per 100 ledgers.

The sign dialog shows the whole request and previews it

The dialog shows every authorization the request asks for

The café's transaction calls the XLM token's transfer with the account as the sender. The token calls require_auth on the account, and because the account is a contract, the host, the program that runs contracts for the network, calls the account's __check_auth to approve [1, 2]. A transaction carries one authorization entry for each approval it needs, and each entry holds a tree of calls, such as a game's guess that itself transfers XLM from the account. Soroban calls each such call a context, and the transaction names a rule for each one; the account checks that rule and searches for no other. The sign dialog decodes every entry from the transaction alone, with no network: it leads with what the user approves in plain words and keeps the whole tree they would authorize under Details.

The whole tree matters because a signed entry is published with its transaction. The café demo's third order comes from a tampered checkout that adds a second entry, a transfer of 4 XLM to another address, which the transaction never uses. If the user signs, anyone can submit that entry later, until it expires; in the recorded run, submitting it alone moved 4 XLM to the other address. The dialog marks the entry as unused, so the user can refuse. A grant that pins the recipient, such as the shop grant, refuses a transfer to any other address, which the dual simulation's "Pay 1 elsewhere" preset shows.

The preview shows the verdict and the budget left

Before the user signs, the dialog previews the request in the simulator. The preview records the transaction independently and flags any entry the request carries that the recording does not need. It then runs the account's own __check_auth with enforcing simulation, which gives Perch's verdict, the change in the account's balance and how much of the grant's budget remains; the recording simulation that finds a transaction's entries skips __check_auth and cannot predict a refusal. In the café demo, a 5 XLM order is accepted, and the budget left falls from 7 to 2 XLM. An 8 XLM order is refused with contract error 5, and the budget stays at 7 XLM.

The budget is the rule's cap. At any ledger the window is the last 100 ledgers, and a transfer is accepted only if the window's total plus the new amount stays within the limit. The account keeps the charges in a spending record in its own storage and charges a transfer in the same decision that checks it. With JavaScript, the cap-window figure plays the shop grant's cap of 10 XLM per 100 ledgers: send amounts and advance the ledger, tick "Check each call alone" to see what a per-call check would let through, or press "Replace the rule" and then send 10 to see a replaced rule start with an empty window.

For the same cap and period, a sliding window is a tighter limit than one that resets on a schedule. MetaMask's delegation framework [3] caps token transfers per fixed period that resets at each period's start (ERC20PeriodTransferEnforcer.sol:11-16), so an app can spend the full cap just before a reset and again just after it. A Perch window stays within its limit wherever it is placed.

If the user refuses, nothing is signed or sent. If the user signs, the wallet submits the transaction and the account decides in a fixed order. Guards first refuse requests the account never allows through a grant, such as a call to its adopted recovery contracts. The account looks up each named rule and its spending record. Its decision function, rule_eval::check_auth in the rule-core library [4], checks every call against its rule and computes the new spending records. It reads no storage and checks no signature, and it is the part the proofs cover. Only if it accepts does a verifier contract check the app's signature, and only if that passes does the account store the new records. A refusal at any step writes nothing. The decision function reports a refusal as contract error 5, or 3 for a grant from before a recovery.

The app signs as an external signer, a key the account checks through a verifier, such as an Ed25519 key or a passkey. Its signature binds the account and the rules the transaction names, so a relayer cannot move the payment to another rule the key belongs to, such as an uncapped one. A delegated signer, another Stellar address that approves through the host, does not bind the rule choice in Perch; OpenZeppelin v0.8.0 binds it for both kinds.

A grant covers the calls a request makes

A request is often a call to another contract that then acts with the account's authority. A guessing game's guess takes a stake: on a loss, the game transfers 1 XLM from the player's account to itself, in a call inside the root call. The account's authorization entry holds that whole tree, and the account checks each call in it against the rule the transaction names for that call. A rule may also name the one root call it serves (docs/auth-root.md). In the nested-call diagram, the pay-game rule allows the XLM token's transfer only from the account, only to the game, only when the entry's root call is exactly game.guess, and at most 2 XLM in any 100 ledgers. The grant works like a keycard that opens the game room, where the game then asks for the stake and the same card must cover it.

The same transfer signed as its own entry has transfer as its root, so pay-game refuses it, as it refuses the transfer under another function or another game, or to anyone but the game. A win needs only the root call, because the game pays the player from its own balance. The stack's tests and the Nido simulation below run each of these cases (auth_root.rs).

A stolen app key meets the same checks

Whoever holds the app's key, the app itself or a thief, goes through the same decision. Under the shop grant that key can send at most 10 XLM per 100 ledgers to the shop and nothing else: no other token, recipient or function, and no call to the account. Any grant it narrows for a helper allows nothing its own grant does not. The owner ends a grant with one call, remove_grant, which deletes its rules and spending records, and a grant's rules also stop at their expiry. If the owner recovers the account after losing a key, one counter on the account advances, and every earlier grant, with every grant narrowed from one, stops authorizing in that same transaction. The vision paper covers the owner's key, guardians and the verifier registry.

The same request runs against an OpenZeppelin account

OpenZeppelin's Soroban account library [5] composes the same decision from separate contracts. Its unit of permission is a context rule: a target such as one contract, an optional expiry, signers, and attached contracts, each a policy the account calls on every call the rule approves. oz-policy-builder [6] writes rules for these accounts. It synthesises a predicate, a condition on one call, from a recorded transaction or a declared intent, and a shared policy contract, the interpreter, evaluates the predicate on every call the rule guards. The interpreter keeps no totals. oz-policy-builder's documentation says limits over time come from a token allowance with an expiry, or from OpenZeppelin's spending_limit policy beside it (docs/stellar.md:404-407).

soroban-sim [7] packages soroban-env-host, the host library the network runs, so this page can run both designs' real contract code offline. It keeps one ledger per design and applies every request with enforcing authorization; its host build does not meter costs exactly. Each side runs contracts built from the pinned sources beside the native XLM contract: the OpenZeppelin v0.8.0 account, oz-policy-builder's policy-interpreter and spending_limit, whose hashes match oz-policy-builder's testnet records; or the Perch account, its document compiler and its Ed25519 verifier. On the OpenZeppelin side the shop rule is the predicate oz-policy-builder's synthesiser writes from the same intent, which pins the recipient and bounds each transfer at 10 XLM, with spending_limit at 10 XLM per 100 ledgers beside it. The interpreter refuses external signers on the rules it polices (error 212), so that side signs as a delegated Stellar account, in a second authorization entry.

Both accounts hold the same rule: the app key may send XLM only to the shop, at most 10 XLM in any 100 ledgers (on the OpenZeppelin side, through spending_limit).

The recorded run shows where the designs differ

The recorded-run figure replays soroban-sim's native run:

  • The app pays the shop 6 XLM, and both accounts apply it. The further 5 XLM is refused by both: Perch in its decision, with no verifier call, and the OpenZeppelin account in spending_limit (error 3221).
  • The owner removes spending_limit from the OpenZeppelin rule. The per-call bound then applies each of three more 6-XLM payments, 18 XLM within a few ledgers, while Perch refuses all three because its window already holds 6. Adding spending_limit back starts it with an empty history, as replacing a Perch rule starts an empty window.
  • A payment to another recipient, a signature by another key in the app's name, and a key not on the rule are refused by both. After 100 ledgers, 5 XLM goes through in both.

A Perch cap is part of its rule, so it cannot be detached on its own; removing it means replacing the rule. A reviewer reads one document and one decision function, rather than the account's checks plus every policy attached to each rule, and one owner call removes a grant across every contract it covers, where an OpenZeppelin account's rules are removed one at a time. A native run, a WASI build and this page return identical replies on every request once timings are removed; Provenance lists every contract's hash. The Aha Company publishes soroban-sim (Apache-2.0), and the authors work there.

Each design is stronger somewhere

oz-policy-builder

  • Maturity. Its interpreter is pinned on testnet, the project is under external audit, its off-chain packages were hardened after a penetration test, and it is exercised on recorded mainnet transactions.
  • Authoring. It tests that each condition of a synthesised predicate is needed and verifies the predicate against the deployed interpreter; a CLI and an MCP server expose its nine tools.
  • Expressiveness and reach. Or, comparisons, conditions over lists, clock windows, slippage floors, reads of other contracts, threshold policies and a deny code for each reason. Its installer supports existing OpenZeppelin account builds, including the 0.7.2 line in use on mainnet (policy-synth/README.md:143).

Perch

  • Limits over time. Every capped rule's decision includes its window.
  • One decision point. One contract holds the rules and their state, installs one document that covers several contracts, and accepts passkey signers on grant rules.
  • Proved decision logic. The decision function is proved in Lean to accept only what the rules allow and to keep each window within its cap.

A Perch grant needs a Perch account.

The companion paper Perch and oz-policy-builder sets out every dimension with a source for each cell, and the oz-policy-builder walkthrough replays one task in both designs step by step.

The decision function's guarantees are proved in Lean

The decision function is about 2,400 lines of Rust. The Charon [8] and Aeneas [9] tools translate it into Lean [10], a proof assistant whose kernel checks every proof, and theorems relate the translation to a readable model written by hand. rule-core's CI regenerates the translation and fails on any difference, and Perch's tests run 57 traces generated from the Lean model through the compiled account, checking every verdict and spending record against the model.

  • The translation behaves as the model does. On every input, the Lean translation of the Rust returns exactly what the model returns, without panicking or looping. If the model refuses the shop grant's second payment of 5 XLM, so does the translated code.
  • An accepted call is always allowed by a rule. If the decision function accepts a request, every call in it is allowed by a rule it was given, under the signers it was given. Under the shop grant, every accepted call is a transfer from the account to the shop that the app's key signed.
  • No window holds more than the cap. However the app times its payments, no window of 100 ledgers holds more than 10 XLM of accepted charges. The cap-window figure is one instance: 6 and 4 XLM fill the window, 1 XLM at ledger t + 99 is refused, and 5 XLM at t + 100 is accepted because the 6 XLM charged at t has left the window.
  • A narrowed grant never allows more. A grant derived by attenuation allows no call its parent does not, under any signers. A helper narrowed from the shop grant can pay no one but the shop.
  • No grant reaches the account itself. No installed grant, and no grant derived from one, allows a call to the account or to the recovery contracts it has adopted. The account refuses at install any rule that targets it, so an app cannot use its grant to install a wider one.
  • Recovery revokes every earlier grant. After a completed recovery, no earlier grant, and no grant derived from one, accepts a request with a call in it. An app that held the shop grant before the recovery can make no further payment through it.

The formal version. What Perch proves states all 13 theorems with their definitions, hypotheses and Lean names, among them the six above (Theorems 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10), and sets out what each one assumes. The PDF edition gives the core results in mathematical form.

A Nido wallet run used a grant end to end

A passkey-signed payment through a grant paid 12,889 stroops in fees, about 0.0013 XLM.

On 5 November 2026, a scripted browser run of the Nido wallet on Stellar testnet, with virtual passkeys, made 11 successful transactions. It installed a game grant whose transfer rule pays only the game, at most 2 XLM per 17,280 ledgers (about a day), made two lost guesses, and completed a one-guardian recovery. The wallet declined a third guess before signing because it would exceed the cap.

The fee, measured on testnet, includes the passkey check; 10,000,000 stroops make one XLM. A fresh account holding the game's grant, after two capped transfers, stores 3,308 bytes and pays 1.677 XLM a year in storage rent at the network's floor rate. We did not measure the OpenZeppelin account's fees or rent.

What Perch shows and what comes next

A Perch grant puts an app's spending limit inside the account's own decision, which Lean proofs show accepts only what the rules allow and keeps each window within its cap, and the sign dialog shows the whole request and its effects before the user signs. In the recorded run, once the owner detached spending_limit, the OpenZeppelin account sent 18 XLM in three payments and Perch refused all three; these were the only verdicts on which the two differed. oz-policy-builder is ahead in maturity, authoring and expressiveness, and Perch should learn from it.

Future work covers an agent policy engine, in which a root agent holds a Perch grant and hands child agents budgets; recording-based authoring, a front end that turns a recorded transaction into a grant document; three more ideas from oz-policy-builder, namely simulating a grant before install, refusing a capped rule that another rule naming the same key could bypass, and testing on a corpus of real mainnet transactions; a bridge from the Cedar policy language to grant documents; and measuring both designs with exact metering. The vision paper describes each. The PDF edition and the companion papers give the evidence behind each claim, the trust assumptions and the limits of the design, and the PDF adds related work.
