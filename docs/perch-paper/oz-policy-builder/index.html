ABSTRACT

Perch and oz-policy-builder bound what an app may do from an OpenZeppelin-style Soroban smart account in two different ways. oz-policy-builder composes a decision from separate contracts attached to the account's rules: a shared interpreter checks each call against a predicate synthesised from a recorded transaction or a declared intent, OpenZeppelin's spending_limit keeps totals, and threshold policies count signers. Perch decides at one point inside the account, with the window cap in the same decision. We compare the two from their public documentation and source at fixed revisions, and from one task run in both in soroban-sim. Perch is stronger where a limit must hold over time, where one place should decide, and where the core check should be proved. oz-policy-builder is stronger in maturity, authoring and expressiveness: a testnet deployment, external review, tests on real mainnet transactions, record-and-synthesise authoring, and live reads, clock windows and quantifiers. We end with what Perch should adopt from it.

At a glance

  1. Shared ground. Both use OpenZeppelin's context rules, both judge each call alone, and both deny by default (§01).
  2. Choose oz-policy-builder today for a design that is deployed on testnet, under external audit and tested on mainnet recordings; that adds rules to OpenZeppelin accounts already in use; for authoring by recording a transaction; and for live reads, clock windows, quantifiers and OpenZeppelin's add-ons (§06).
  3. Consider Perch for a spending window inside the decision, one place that decides, and a core check with machine-checked proofs. In return you take a design that is unmerged, runs on testnet only as a preview and has no audit (§07).
  4. Run both. The simulation page runs both accounts' real contract code side by side, offline, on requests you choose.

01Two ways to bound an app

Perch and oz-policy-builder bound what an app or agent may do from an OpenZeppelin-style Soroban account. oz-policy-builder composes the bound from separate contracts; Perch decides it at one point inside the account.

This paper compares the two designs in both directions: where oz-policy-builder is stronger, where Perch is, and what Perch should take from oz-policy-builder. It is a companion to the Perch paper, which describes Perch's grants and what is proved about them, but it stands alone and repeats what it needs.

What we read, what we ran

We read oz-policy-builder [1] at 43b13ed (2026-10-30): release v5.2.0 plus 17 later commits, none under its contracts/ directory. We read OpenZeppelin's Soroban account library [2] at stellar-contracts v0.8.0, and Perch [3] at f0275f4 with its evaluator in rule-core [4] at cbabe59. A path such as docs/stellar.md:404-407 names a file and lines in oz-policy-builder at its pin; oz8: marks OpenZeppelin v0.8.0, and Perch paths are at Perch's pin. Appendix A gives a source for every cell of the comparison.

We also ran oz-policy-builder. We built it at the pin, and its 575 Rust test runs and 1,823 TypeScript tests passed. We ran its record, synthesise, verify and install flow against an OpenZeppelin v0.8.0 account on a local Stellar quickstart network (protocol 30). And we ran one task through both designs in soroban-sim [5]: in the walkthrough, and in the dual simulation that the Perch paper records and the simulation page reruns (§03). None of these runs measures cost.

Disclosure. The authors designed Perch and work at The Aha Company, which also publishes soroban-sim (theahaco/soroban-sim, Apache-2.0). We are a party to this comparison. We describe oz-policy-builder only from its public documentation and source, and where a claim rests on our reading or our runs rather than its documents, the text says so.

Shared ground

  • The same rule model. OpenZeppelin's account checks each context against the context rule the signer names for it in context_rule_ids, with External or Delegated signers. oz-policy-builder attaches to those rules; Perch reproduces the field and the signer kinds and attaches nothing.
  • Each call is judged alone. The interpreter "sees one authorised call at a time" (docs/architecture.md:307-309), and Perch checks each context against its own rule. In both, a rule written for the transfer a game makes also allows that transfer made directly.
  • Both deny by default. The interpreter is "fail-closed" (README.md:21-24) and its predicates have no not. A Perch account accepts a context only when the rule named for it allows it.
  • Neither lets an app's rule reach the account's administration. The interpreter refuses a Default scope, or a rule scoped to the account or to itself (error 218; policy-interpreter/src/lib.rs:103-114). Perch refuses a grant rule whose target is the account, its adopted recovery controller, or the zero-knowledge adapter and pool of its recovery configuration (Theorem 8).

The trade-off

oz-policy-builder composes. A shared interpreter checks each call against a stored predicate, OpenZeppelin's spending_limit keeps totals, and a threshold policy counts signers. Each is a contract that can be reused, replaced and audited on its own, and a new combination of constraints is data, not code. In exchange, a property that spans calls or signers holds only while the right policy is attached, and the decision's state lives in several contracts. Perch puts one document behind one decision point: the rules, spending records and grant generation live in the account, and one function decides. In exchange, its rule language is smaller and its implementation is younger and unaudited.

02Where each design decides

An OpenZeppelin account with oz-policy-builder authenticates the signers and then asks each attached policy; a Perch account decides first, in one function, and authenticates after.

oz-policy-builder in two halves

Off chain, a synthesiser (@crediolabs/policy-synth) records a transaction or takes a declared Intent, writes a predicate in the interpreter's wire form, dry-runs it through the interpreter's own check, renders a review, and returns an unsigned install transaction for the owner's wallet. A command-line tool and an MCP server expose the same nine tools (README.md:13-24). On chain, one shared policy-interpreter contract, attached to an OpenZeppelin context rule as a policy, evaluates the stored predicate in enforce on every guarded call (contracts/stellar/policy-interpreter/src/lib.rs:192-277). Much of docs/stellar.md describes Prime, a custody product built on these parts; the interpreter and the synthesiser are what correspond to Perch's engine.

Where the OpenZeppelin account decides

An OpenZeppelin context rule has a scope, an optional valid_until ledger, signers and policies; a policy is a separate contract with the hooks install, uninstall and enforce. In v0.8.0 the account checks that each selected rule exists, matches its context and has not expired; refuses a signer on none of the selected rules; authenticates the signers; and then calls each attached policy's enforce (oz8:storage.rs:629-690), in the rule's stored order: address order for the policies the rule was created with, then any added later (oz8:storage.rs:830, 1304). A policy that refuses rolls back the whole call, including what earlier policies wrote.

Where the Perch account decides

A Perch account attaches no policies. Its __check_auth runs a few guards and then the evaluator, rule_eval::check_auth, linked into the account. The evaluator takes the selected rules, their spending records, the contexts, one rule index per context, the claimed signers, the ledger and the grant generation, and returns the next records or a refusal; it reads no storage and checks no signature. Only after it accepts does the account authenticate each signer through its verifier contract, and only then does it write the records (crates/perch-smart-account/src/gate.rs:56-123). Perch's deployed build c135aca used the attached-policy shape on a fork of OpenZeppelin's account library; the design compared here moved the decision into the account.

Three more ideas from oz-policy-builder

  • Predicate. The interpreter's stored expression for one (account, rule) pair. It has no not, and its ne and nin deny when an operand does not resolve (policy-interpreter/README.md:632-639).
  • Execution adapter. A contract that runs a batch of calls atomically, because Soroban allows one host-function invocation per transaction. A predicate on its execute can relate the calls in the batch (docs/stellar.md:191-195).
  • Keyless rule. A rule with no signer, which the interpreter admits only when it is bound to an execution adapter; anyone may then run what the predicate permits (lib.rs:210-223). Perch refuses a rule with no signer.

Figure 1 follows one guarded call through both accounts in three scenarios under the game grant of Figure 3. The game, Guess the Number [6], takes 1 XLM from the player's account for each wrong guess, through a transfer it makes; the grant lets the app key call guess and send XLM only to the game, at most 2 XLM per 17,280 ledgers. Over the cap, Perch refuses in its decision (auth error 5), before any signer is authenticated; the OpenZeppelin account refuses in spending_limit, after. The figure is read from source.

03One task, run in both

One task ran through both designs in soroban-sim. In the walkthrough's seven requests both gave the same verdict, differing in which contracts took part and where each refused; in the dual simulation they differ only after the owner removes spending_limit.

The task: an app key may send XLM from the account only to the shop, at most 10 XLM in any 100-ledger window. The walkthrough runs it in soroban-sim, one session per design: an OpenZeppelin v0.8.0 account whose rule carries the predicate oz-policy-builder's synthesiser writes from the task's declared Intent, with spending_limit beside the interpreter; and a Perch account holding one grant. The signers differ by design. oz-policy-builder accepts only delegated signers on the rules it polices, so the OpenZeppelin side signs as a delegated Stellar account, whose approval rides in a second authorization entry; the Perch side signs with an Ed25519 key that a verifier contract checks.

A per-call bound alone

The interpreter keeps no totals (docs/stellar.md:404-407). We ran that configuration once, outside the walkthrough: on a local Stellar quickstart network (protocol 30), an OpenZeppelin account whose rule carried only the interpreter, with a per-call bound of 5 XLM, applied three sends that moved 9 XLM, and refused one of 5 XLM and a stroop. This is the documented behaviour. The run was not archived; the recorded evidence for the same point is the dual simulation.

The same point, recorded and rerunnable

The dual simulation in the Perch paper runs the shop task with the real contract code of both designs in one host, offline; its native, WASI and browser runs give the same replies, wall-clock timings aside. The 6-XLM payment applies in both designs, and the 5 XLM after it is refused in both: by Perch in its decision, with no verifier call, and by the OpenZeppelin account in spending_limit (3221). The owner then removes spending_limit, and the interpreter's per-call bound of 10 XLM applies three 6-XLM sends (18 XLM). Perch refuses all three, because its window already holds 6. Re-adding spending_limit starts an empty history, as replacing a Perch rule starts an empty window. On the simulation page a reader can change the amounts, the recipient, the signer and the ledger, and remove or restore spending_limit.

04One intent, two spellings

Written in both languages, one intent shows where they differ: where the window lives, how rules are installed, who may sign, and what a rule may consult.

Figure 3 writes one intent both ways. It is the game grant that the Nido wallet's testnet preview installed: the app key may call guess on the game, and may send XLM only to the game, at most 2 XLM per 17,280 ledgers (one nominal day), through ledger N − 1. We read the oz-policy-builder side from source and did not run its installs. The numbered notes match the marks in the figure.

  1. Both systems select one rule per context, and each rule sees its own call alone (docs/architecture.md:307-309). So in both, a transfer to the game is allowed without a guess.
  2. In Perch the window cap is part of the rule. oz-policy-builder turns the Intent's cap into a per-call lte and attaches spending_limit for the window, because the interpreter keeps no totals (contracts/stellar/policy-interpreter/src/dsl.rs:65-72). A Perch document cannot bound a single call's amount; its window limit is its only amount bound.
  3. Perch installs both rules in one transaction. oz-policy-builder builds one add_context_rule per install_policy call (packages/policy-synth/src/tools.ts:588-590).
  4. Perch's grant rule holds a passkey as an external signer. oz-policy-builder writes every rule signer as Delegated (install.ts:148), and the interpreter refuses External signers with error 212, so an oz-policy-builder app signs as a Stellar or contract address. Its install is authorized by sourceAccount, which must be a signer of rule 0, the owners' rule; a Perch install needs the owner's authorization in the account's own entry.
  5. Expiry: in Perch, not-after-ledger N authorizes through N − 1. OpenZeppelin's valid_until authorizes through itself (oz8:storage.rs:321-322), so validUntilLedger: N − 1 names the same last ledger.
  6. oz-policy-builder can consult live state, for example a read of a registry before permitting a recipient (docs/stellar.md:369-375); a Perch rule reads nothing outside the account. A Perch grant ends when the account recovers. oz-policy-builder has no account-key recovery; the "recovery" of its Prime product moves custody funds to a fixed address (docs/architecture.md:81-94).

Starting from a recording

oz-policy-builder can also start from a recorded transaction. Its registry of known protocols turns a recording into an Intent: who is pinned, what is capped, what is asked (packages/policy-synth/README.md:50-51). For a function the registry does not know, every argument that is not an amount is pinned to its recorded value (src/synth.ts:62-86). guess is not in the registry, so a recorded lost guess would pin the guessed number; we derived this from source and did not run it. Verification then puts the rule to the deployed interpreter's check, derives deny cases, and drops each conjunct in turn to test minimality, listing what it could not examine (policy-synth/README.md:52-64). A recording shows one call. It cannot show a total, an expiry or the account's other rules, which is why spendingLimit and validUntilLedger are install inputs and the overlap scan reads every live rule on the account (policy-synth/README.md:176).

05Safety by composition

oz-policy-builder gets its guarantees by composition: each contract checks one thing, and the account runs them together. That is a deliberate trade, and it buys flexibility.

What oz-policy-builder documents

  • Rules judge one call. "A Stellar call that authorises two contexts (a Blend submit and its token transfer) needs a rule for each, because the interpreter sees one authorised call at a time" (docs/architecture.md:307-309). The remedy for relating calls is the execution adapter: a rule bound to the adapter is honoured only for a call in a batch the adapter checked, and a predicate on the adapter's execute can relate the calls of that batch (docs/stellar.md:191-195).
  • Totals come from another policy. The interpreter "does not see what a transaction moved, keep rolling totals or count calls. Amount limits over time come from the allowance and its expiry, or from OpenZeppelin's spending_limit beside the interpreter" (docs/stellar.md:404-407). The synthesiser attaches spending_limit only on transfer, because that policy refuses every other call (packages/policy-synth/src/install.ts:957-967). Its window has the same boundary as Perch's, (C − P, C], with at most 815 entries (oz8:policies/spending_limit.rs:9-12, 169). It is outside oz-policy-builder's audit scope (docs/audit-overview.md:76-85), but OpenZeppelin publishes audit reports for its library, and its v0.8.0 diff audit lists policies/spending_limit.rs and smart_account/storage.rs in scope (oz8:audits/, v0.8.0 report, p. 4).
  • Several signers need a threshold policy. "An install with several signers needs a threshold (OpenZeppelin simple_threshold or weighted_threshold) that one signer cannot meet alone. Without one, any single signer could act alone" (README.md:122-125). The tool refuses such an install unless the owners set anyOne.
  • Changing and ending a rule are separate steps. The interpreter stores a hash of a policed rule's signer set and refuses with error 204 once the set changes, so changing who signs means adding a new rule and removing the old one, or removing the policy and adding it again with the policy admins' authorization (docs/stellar.md:552-554). Rules are removed one at a time. Removing a rule with only rule 0's signatures leaves the interpreter's entry for it until the entry archives, because the interpreter's uninstall needs the policy admins' own authorization; the account gives every new rule a fresh ID and never reads that entry again (docs/stellar.md:548-562).

What composition buys

The README states the aim: "one audit-once engine, and every policy is declarative data fed into it, so a new combination of constraints needs no new contract and no re-audit" (README.md:21-24). The interpreter has no admin and no upgrade path (docs/architecture.md:153).

Composition also lets a rule consult the world. A predicate can read another contract during enforce, for example an oracle price or a registry's list, up to the instance's max_reads, 8 on the testnet pin (docs/stellar.md:369-394). oz-policy-builder documents the care this needs: a read sees state after anything the submitter ran earlier in the same transaction, so a rule should read only values the submitter cannot move and restore. And because the decision is assembled from OpenZeppelin policies, OpenZeppelin's threshold policies and spending_limit, which other OpenZeppelin accounts also run, sit beside the interpreter.

What Perch does instead, and what it costs

A Perch grant is one document, lowered into rules that the account stores with their spending records and decides with one function: target, functions, argument conditions, threshold, expiry and window cap together, before any signer is authenticated.

The costs are real. The rule language is small: five conjunctive argument conditions, no or and no not, no reads of other contracts, and no bound on a single call's amount. A condition the language lacks needs new evaluator code, new proofs and a new account build, which a deployed account reaches only through its own upgrade, scheduled by the owner and delayed 120,960 ledgers (seven days at the nominal five-second ledger). Nothing can be swapped in: an account cannot attach a policy someone else wrote and audited, and OpenZeppelin's add-ons do not apply to grant rules. And the one place that decides must be right, which is why the proofs and their limits matter here (§07).

06Where oz-policy-builder is stronger

oz-policy-builder is ahead on maturity, on authoring, and on what a rule can say and plug into. These are the reasons to choose it today.

Maturity

oz-policy-builder's packages pin one interpreter instance on testnet, CDER6P73JWMIVD3X6TGGOQIDAH3DLU7OKRTLT4ULPTZ3QJJ3QUGTK7CM, running the Wasm built from this tree (6704996f…); they pin none on mainnet yet (packages/policy-synth/src/config.ts:57-80). An external audit of release v5.2.0 has been in progress since 26 October 2026, covering the interpreter, custody gate, execution adapter, Prime seat and three TypeScript packages at v5.2.0, with the OpenZeppelin account and its policies out of scope (README.md:57-59, 166-171). Its off-chain packages were hardened after an external penetration test (pull request #78, fix/hacken-pt-findings, whose merge commit is the pin, 43b13ed).

It is tested on real transactions. A corpus test takes 33 mainnet contract calls of 26 shapes, among them SEP-41 transfers, Soroswap swaps and Blend submits, from decoding through a synthesised rule to the interpreter's verdict on the call it came from, replayed offline (packages/policy-synth/src/corpus.test.ts:1-3; fixtures/corpus/mainnet.json). The interpreter has 440 tests, 26 of them against OpenZeppelin's v0.8.0 account (docs/audit-overview.md:15-20); a worst-case suite runs its Wasm; scripted testnet runs cover the executor binding, the adapter, a no-gate liquidation and the v0.8.0 migration (evidence/README.md:10, 18-24); and mutation testing of one path gives 31 of 32 killed, the survivor equivalent (evidence/README.md:23). A STRIDE threat register gives each threat its control, how it was checked and its status (evidence/README.md:17).

Perch is behind on each count. The design compared here is an unmerged stack of pull requests ending at #68, runs on testnet as a preview through the Nido wallet, and has had no external audit. Its Wasm runs in release-stack tests, a scripted reference run and 11 testnet transactions; there is no corpus of real transactions. Perch's earlier design, c135aca, is on mainnet.

Authoring

oz-policy-builder starts from what an app did. Its synthesiser takes a recorded transaction, or a declared Intent, and writes a predicate that permits it. It verifies the result by dry-running the deployed interpreter's own check, so one evaluator both verifies and enforces, and it tests that each conjunct is needed by dropping each in turn. It reads every live rule on the account and refuses an install a shared signer could bypass through a looser neighbour, or whose rolling total such a neighbour would escape (packages/policy-synth/README.md:171). It renders a review for the owner, which names any override the owners chose, and returns unsigned install bytes. All of this is nine tools over a command-line tool and an MCP server, published to npm at 5.2.0 as three packages (README.md:13-24; packages/*/package.json:3).

Perch starts from what the owner grants. A wallet or app writes a grant document, with the perch-js builder or its requestToPolicyDoc for an ERC-7715-shaped request, and the account's document compiler validates it at install. Perch's perch-analyze crate runs offline checks on documents, but Perch has no recorder and no minimality check, and checking a grant against the account's own evaluator before install is one of the proposals in §08.

Expressiveness and ecosystem

oz-policy-builder adds rules to accounts already in use, with no new account code. Its installer signs for the v0.7.2-line build that existing Prime and mainnet accounts run, and for v0.8.0 (packages/policy-synth/README.md:143; deployments/oz-accounts.json:2). A Perch grant needs a Perch account.

A predicate can say more than a Perch rule. Its grammar has and and or, six comparisons, in and nin, the quantifiers all, any and sum over vectors, checked scaled arithmetic, the ledger clock now, nested paths into arguments, and read of other contracts (contracts/stellar/policy-interpreter/src/dsl.rs:84-158), up to 8 reads per enforce on the testnet pin. An Intent can state clock windows (until, from), a swap's slippage floor (minOut), and properties of every element, some element or the total of a list (each, some, total) (packages/policy-synth/src/intent.ts:18-49). A Perch rule has a target, a function list, five conjunctive argument conditions, a threshold, an expiry and a window cap, and reads nothing outside the account.

It also fits the rest of the OpenZeppelin ecosystem. Thresholds come from OpenZeppelin's simple_threshold and weighted_threshold policies (README.md:122-125). A refusal reports a deny code per reason (contracts/stellar/policy-interpreter/src/storage.rs:27-86), where a Perch refusal reports only auth error 3 or 5 and a wallet re-runs the evaluator off chain to learn which check failed. And OpenZeppelin v0.8.0 binds the selected rule IDs for delegated signers as well as external ones (oz8:storage.rs:157-175, 396-412); Perch binds them only for external signers, because a delegated signer approves the host's payload, which does not name them. OpenZeppelin's spending_limit can change its limit in place and keep its history (oz8:policies/spending_limit.rs:340-365; the example contract exposes it, oz8:examples/multisig-smart-account/spending-limit-policy/src/contract.rs:79-85); changing a Perch cap starts an empty record (Perch docs/DEFERRED.md:298-324).

The design also has shapes Perch lacks. A keyless rule bound to an execution adapter lets anyone run, for example, a liquidation the predicate permits (docs/architecture.md:149). A rule may follow another account's rule, so an issuer governs a borrower's limits (docs/audit-overview.md:249-254). And an adapter batch can wait before it runs, while one predicate relates its calls (docs/stellar.md:125-140, 350-352).

07Where Perch is stronger

Perch is ahead where a limit must hold over time, where one place should decide, and where the core check should be proved. Each point comes with what it does not cover.

A limit over time, inside the decision

A Perch rule may carry a cap of λ per P ledgers, which the evaluator charges in the same decision that checks the call; a refusal changes nothing. Theorem 6 proves that, from empty records, the accepted charges to a capped rule in any window (L − P, L] sum to at most λ, however the app times its payments. The bound belongs to the rule, so it holds while the rule keeps its cap.

In oz-policy-builder the total is a separate, opt-in policy. OpenZeppelin's spending_limit keeps a window with the same boundary, but nothing in the interpreter ties a rule to it (though the installer refuses a total a shared signer could bypass), and the synthesiser attaches it only on transfer (docs/stellar.md:404-407; packages/policy-synth/src/install.ts:957-967). The recorded dual simulation shows the difference: after the owner removes spending_limit, the per-call bound applies three 6-XLM sends and 18 XLM leave the account, while Perch refuses all three (§03). The owner chose that configuration, and oz-policy-builder documents it; the difference is where the guarantee lives.

What it does not cover. The theorem is about the evaluator's Lean translation, from empty records; rule selection and record persistence in the account are tested. Caps are per rule, not per grant, and replacing a capped rule starts an empty window. One spending record holds at most 907 spends per window. A Perch document cannot bound a single call's amount.

One decision point

  • State in one contract. The rules, the spending records and the grant generation are in the account's storage; no other contract keeps part of the decision.
  • Decide, then authenticate. The whole decision, cap included, runs before any verifier is called, so a refused request calls no verifier, and nothing is written unless the signatures also check (crates/perch-smart-account/src/gate.rs:93-113).
  • One install across contracts. A grant whose rules name several contracts installs with one apply_grant call; the threshold is part of each rule.
  • Passkeys on grant rules. A grant rule may hold an external signer, such as a passkey checked by a WebAuthn verifier contract, or a delegated one. oz-policy-builder's policed rules take delegated signers only: its interpreter authorizes signers with require_auth, which an external signer's verifier never satisfies (policy-interpreter/src/lib.rs:451-462).

What it does not cover. One decision point is also one place that must be right. A delegated signer does not bind the selected rule IDs. A refusal reports only one of two codes. And an account cannot add someone else's policy beside its rules.

A proved core

The decision function, about 2,400 lines of no_std Rust in rule-core, is translated into Lean by Charon [7] and Aeneas [8]. Lean's kernel [9] has checked that the translation returns what a readable model returns on every input, without panicking or looping (Theorem 1); that when the decision function accepts, each call is allowed by some rule it was given (Theorem 2); that attenuation never widens the calls a grant allows (Theorem 4); the window bound (Theorem 6); and that no installed grant, nor one derived from it, allows a call to the account or its adopted recovery contracts (Theorem 8). What Perch proves has all thirteen theorems. For oz-policy-builder, the evidence is tests, partly on the shipped Wasm, and a STRIDE register; we found no formal model in its tree.

What it does not cover. The proofs are about the Lean translation, not the compiled Wasm, and nothing checks one against the other. The account code around the function is tested. Attenuation is modelled and proved, but the account cannot install an attenuated grant at the pinned build. Nothing is audited.

Stopping every earlier grant

Each installed grant is stamped with the account's grant generation, which only a completed recovery advances. After one, every earlier grant stops authorizing through the account: it refuses every request with a call in it, from the completion transaction on. Allowances a grant created stay usable. This is proved in rule-core's account model (Theorem 10), tested, and run on testnet (the Perch vision). OpenZeppelin rules are removed one at a time. What it does not cover: the account model has no Rust counterpart, passing the generation in is tested shell code, and outside a recovery the owner also removes Perch grants one at a time.

08What Perch should learn

Six things oz-policy-builder or OpenZeppelin does that Perch should adopt. All six are proposals; none is built.

  1. Recording as authoring. We propose a front end that records what an app did and emits a Perch grant document for the owner to review, as oz-policy-builder's synthesiser emits a predicate. The document would stay the object the owner reads and signs, and the account's compiler and lowering would still check it at install. An owner may not know the exact contracts, functions and recipients an app needs; a recording shows them.
  2. Simulate before install, against the deployed evaluator. oz-policy-builder verifies a predicate by dry-running the deployed interpreter's own check, so the evaluator that verifies is the one that enforces. We propose the same for Perch: before the owner signs an install, apply the grant in an enforcing simulation of the account and run the app's expected requests through the account's own __check_auth. soroban-sim already runs the real account offline; the same driver could run in a wallet.
  3. Minimality checks. We propose flagging any rule wider than the recording it came from: a longer function list than the recording used, an argument left unconstrained, a cap above what the recording moved, or no expiry. oz-policy-builder drops each conjunct in turn to show that it is needed; Perch could show the owner the same for each field. And, as oz-policy-builder's installer does, we propose refusing a capped rule that a key it names could bypass through another rule, since a Perch key holds the union of every rule naming it.
  4. Keep a record across a limit change. OpenZeppelin's spending_limit changes its limit and keeps its history. We propose keeping a rule's spending record when only its limit changes; today any change to a cap starts an empty record.
  5. A corpus of real transactions. We propose running real mainnet calls, of the kinds oz-policy-builder's corpus test holds (SEP-41 transfers, Soroswap swaps, Blend submits), through Perch's lowering and evaluator. That would show which intents a grant document cannot express and where the evaluator refuses a call an owner meant to allow.
  6. An external audit before claiming maturity. We propose an external audit of the Perch contracts (account, document compiler, recovery controller, verifiers, zero-knowledge pool and adapter, registry) and of rule-core's evaluator, and merging the open pull requests, before Perch is described as ready. Until then the authors do not recommend Perch for real funds.

The Perch paper's future-work pointer and the Perch vision list these with Perch's other proposals.

10What this comparison does not show

This comparison reads two designs and runs one task. It measures no costs, and some things must not be read into it.

What the evidence covers

  • Every design claim about oz-policy-builder is read from its public documentation and source at 43b13ed. Our runs cover its test suites, its install flow on a local network, one per-call run that was not archived, the walkthrough's task and the recorded dual simulation.
  • Both runs of the shared task used one host version, soroban-env-host 29.0.2 in soroban-sim, in its recording-mode build rather than the exact-metering one, and different signer kinds on the two sides. They show verdicts and call structure, not cost.
  • The OpenZeppelin 0.7.2-line account build that oz-policy-builder also signs for (91a2cd56…) has no recorded source; claims about it come from oz-policy-builder's documentation.
  • Only one task ran in both designs; no swap, lending or custody task, where oz-policy-builder's reads, quantifiers and adapter matter, was run.

A comparative measurement (proposed)

We propose, and have not run, five workloads in one harness, soroban-sim with one host version, exact metering and Ed25519 signers, reporting CPU instructions, memory, ledger I/O entries and bytes, fees, and cross-contract calls counted from diagnostic events:

  1. W1, one capped transfer;
  2. W2, a losing guess, a root call and its child;
  3. W3, one install;
  4. W4, ending authority over k grants: one recovery completion against k calls to remove_context_rule;
  5. W5, storage after n transfers.

The walkthrough and the dual simulation are not W1: they ran without exact metering, with different signers.

What we do not claim

  • That Perch is cheaper, faster or smaller than oz-policy-builder, or makes fewer calls. No such measurement exists, and the call structures in Figure 2 reflect the signer kinds as well as the designs.
  • Any comparison of code size; the two projects count lines differently.
  • That oz-policy-builder is unproven. We found no formal model in its tree; its evidence is of another kind.
  • That oz-policy-builder runs on mainnet. Its packages pin an interpreter on testnet only; OpenZeppelin's v0.7.2 policies are on mainnet.
  • That Perch binds more in general. For delegated signers OpenZeppelin binds the rule IDs and Perch does not.
  • That Perch's recovery is an advantage over oz-policy-builder. They address different things: Perch replaces an account's lost credentials, and Prime's "recovery" moves custody funds.
  • That the absence in Perch of the items oz-policy-builder discloses about keyless and following rules is a Perch security property. Those items come from features Perch does not have.
  • That spending_limit and Perch's cap behave alike beyond the eviction boundary they share.

11Related work

Composition is the common pattern on EVM chains too; synthesis from recordings has a long history in access control.

ERC-7579 [10] accounts install validators and hooks as modules. Smart Sessions, a Rhinestone and Biconomy module for such accounts, restricts a session key through policies [11], and MetaMask's delegation framework restricts a delegation through caveat enforcer contracts [12]. Each, like oz-policy-builder, assembles a bound from separate contracts. Synthesising a rule from recorded calls relates to mining access-control policies from logs [13].

12Conclusion

In the recorded dual run the two designs differed only where the owner removed spending_limit.

oz-policy-builder is ahead in maturity, authoring and expressiveness, and it works on OpenZeppelin accounts already in use. Perch keeps a limit over time inside one decision whose core is proved, at the cost of a smaller rule language and a design that is unmerged and unaudited. The lessons in §08 are proposed work.
